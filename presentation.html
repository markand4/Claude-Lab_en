Overview · The Big Picture

The Central Argument

📌 The Reversal

The US used to be the global benchmark for competitive markets — cheaper internet, cheaper flights, cheaper phone service than Europe. Sometime around 2000, that flipped. Americans now pay nearly double what Europeans pay for broadband.

📌 The Cause

Philippon argues this is not driven by globalization or technology. It is a domestic political failure — decades of corporate lobbying systematically weakened the enforcement mechanisms meant to protect competition.

📌 The Stakes

US consumers lose an estimated $65 billion per year on mobile service alone relative to German consumers. Rising profit margins — from 6–7% of GDP to ~10% — represent a structural shift flowing to shareholders, not workers.

Overview · Chapter Breakdown

What Each Chapter Does

Intro + Ch. 1 — Why Competition Matters

Sets up the puzzle: the US was once the competitive benchmark. Ch. 1 makes the economic case — competition lowers prices, raises wages, encourages investment, and reduces inequality.

Ch. 2 — The Measurement Problem

Not all concentration is bad. Walmart dominance → prices fell 30%. The chapter introduces the tools: HHI index, profit margins, price trends. Key lesson: you need all three moving together before you can judge.

Ch. 3 — The Evidence

Concentration has risen in >75% of US industries since the late 1990s. Probability of a top firm being displaced in 3 years dropped from 45% → 30%. Six competing explanations are tested; "decreasing domestic competition" wins.

Analysis · What Stood Out

Interesting, Surprising & Debatable

🌍 Surprising — The Europe Flip

We assume European markets are sluggish and over-regulated. Philippon flips it with data: European consumers get cheaper, faster internet and more competitive airline markets. France's Free Mobile entered in 2011 and cut average prices by 40% in six months. The US never got that moment.

⚖️ Interesting — Profits ≠ Efficiency

Philippon is not anti-profit. The critical distinction is profit from efficiency vs. profit from market power (rent extraction). Walmart's dominance was pro-consumer. Amazon's is murkier — it hasn't cut prices the same way and the Diapers.com story raises predatory pricing concerns.

📊 Also Surprising — Entrenchment Data

Every CEO says "competition has never been tougher." The persistence data says the opposite — market leaders are more entrenched today than 20 years ago, not less. It is now substantially easier to predict who the market leader will be in 5 years.

🔁 Worth Pushing Back On

Some economists argue rising markups reflect intangible capital (software, brand value) not anti-competitive behavior. The "superstar firms" explanation is dismissed but not universally rejected. And the Amazon section feels unresolved — does his framework fully capture harm via foreclosed future competition?

Discussion · Question 1 of 5

The Amazon Problem

Question 1

Walmart dominating retail in the 90s was fine because prices dropped. Airlines post-2010 — bad concentration. So where does Amazon land? It hasn't cut prices the way Walmart did, it killed Diapers.com through what looks like predatory pricing, and regulators let it happen. Is Amazon a superstar firm or the next AT&T? And does Philippon's framework even give you the tools to tell the difference?

Superstar Firm View

Amazon built genuinely superior logistics, Prime ecosystem, and AWS. Consumers choose it freely. Dominance earned through innovation.

Market Power View

Predatory pricing to eliminate rivals, then leveraging dominance. Harm isn't higher prices today — it's foreclosed competition tomorrow.

Discussion · Question 2 of 5

Genius or Gatekeeper?

Question 2

Google, Amazon, Meta — these companies built real things. So is their market dominance because they are that good, or because they made it impossible for anyone else to compete? And the harder version: can both be true at the same time? If yes — what does smart policy even look like?

Why the Distinction Matters

Policy response differs entirely. If it's genuine innovation, intervention risks destroying value. If it's market power, inaction destroys value and transfers it upward to shareholders.

The Hard Policy Question

If both are true simultaneously — break up the firm and lose scale efficiencies, or regulate it like a utility and risk stifling innovation?

Discussion · Question 3 of 5

The Europe Comparison

Question 3

We always hear Europe is over-regulated, slow, anti-innovation. But Philippon shows Europe is more competitive than the US in telecom, internet, and airlines. So either the story we've been told about Europe is wrong — or there's something specific about how the US went off the rails that isn't about regulation at all. Which is it? And does that change what you think the US needs to fix?

Possible Explanation A

European antitrust enforcement remained stricter and less captured by lobbying. The US narrative about European sluggishness was always ideological, not empirical.

Possible Explanation B

The US uniquely allowed corporate political spending to hollow out competition enforcement — a problem specific to US campaign finance, not regulation per se.

Discussion · Question 4 of 5

Regulatory Capture

Question 4

Regulatory capture describes a situation where industries influence the agencies meant to oversee them. Can you think of current examples beyond the ones Philippon cites? Is there a structural way to prevent this — or is it an inevitable feature of democratic governance with powerful private interests?

The Capture Mechanism

The problem can't easily be fixed within the current political system — because that system has been compromised by the very firms that benefit from weak competition enforcement.

Campaign Finance Link

Philippon argues corporate political spending is a root cause of declining competition. Are antitrust and campaign finance actually the same issue at different levels?

Discussion · Question 5 of 5

Why Don't Markets Self-Correct?

Question 5

If rising market concentration is harming consumers and workers, why haven't market forces corrected this? Why haven't new competitors entered these industries to capture those profits? What structural barriers prevent the textbook competitive response?

Barriers to Entry

  • Network effects lock in incumbents
  • Regulatory barriers raised by lobbying
  • Capital requirements and patent walls
  • Incumbents acquire would-be rivals early

The Deeper Issue

If the market can't self-correct and political channels are captured, what legitimate levers remain? This is where Philippon's argument is most radical — and most uncomfortable.

Thank You

The Great Reversal argues that the US doesn't have a market problem — it has a politics problem. Competition policy is broken not by accident, but by design of the interests that benefit from its failure.
